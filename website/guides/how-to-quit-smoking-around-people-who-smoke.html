How to quit smoking when people around you still smoke

Friends, family, partners, and coworkers can be part of your life and part of your smoking routine. A few clear requests and a plan for difficult moments can help you protect your effort without needing everyone else to make the same choice.

By DecraveUpdated September 30, 20266-minute read

Seeing someone smoke, being offered a cigarette, or joining a familiar smoke break can bring up an urge. The CDC recommends telling people you are quitting and asking for specific support, such as not smoking around you or offering you cigarettes.

You do not have to avoid every person who smokes forever. You can decide which situations feel manageable now, which ones need a different plan, and what kind of help you want from the people close to you.

Ask for the support you actually need

Make the request specific and easy to understand. You could say:

  • “I’m trying not to smoke. Please don’t offer me cigarettes, even if I ask in the moment.”
  • “Could we meet somewhere smoke-free for a while?”
  • “I’m going to skip the smoke break. I’ll come back in a few minutes.”
  • “I still want to spend time with you; I’m changing this part of the plan so it’s easier for me.”

You can keep the explanation short. You are asking for a practical adjustment, not asking someone else to defend or change their own choices.

Make a plan for the moments you share

Before a gathering

Think about where people usually smoke, whether alcohol or a familiar routine is involved, and how you will leave or take a break if an urge becomes difficult. If a particular event feels too hard right now, suggest another activity or choose to skip this one. Temporary changes can give you space to practice other responses.

When someone offers you a cigarette

Prepare one sentence so you do not have to improvise: “No thanks, I’m not smoking.” Then change the subject, move to another spot, or join someone who is not smoking. You do not need to debate your decision.

For routines at home or work

Ask for a concrete change, such as not smoking around you, keeping cigarettes and lighters out of a shared area, or taking your break somewhere else together. If the request is not possible, plan a separate route, break, or place to meet. Avoid relying on a closed door or another indoor room as a substitute for a smoke-free environment.

Keep an easy exit available

For an early gathering or a difficult event, arrange your own way home or agree on a signal with someone supportive. Knowing you can step away can make it easier to stay connected to your plan.

If the people around you are not ready to change

You cannot control whether someone else smokes, but you can choose when and where you spend time, what support you request, and what you will do if an offer or smoke break becomes a trigger. You might meet in a different place, take a short break, or leave and reconnect later.

If someone close to you also wants to quit, you can encourage each other without requiring identical plans or timelines. A quitline or healthcare professional can offer support tailored to you. In the United States, call 1-800-QUIT-NOW for free quit-smoking support.

When a social trigger turns into a craving

Notice the cue and choose one next action: move away from the smoking area, get a drink of water, contact a supportive person, or return to the activity you came for. An urge can still be present while you make a different choice. For more ideas, read what to do when a craving hits and how to identify your smoking triggers.

If you do smoke, treat it as information about the situation rather than a reason to give up. Our guide explains how to keep your progress after a slip.

Decrave lets you record a craving and note a trigger such as a person, place, routine, or feeling. Reviewing those notes can help you decide what request or alternative plan to try next time. It is habit support, not medical treatment or a substitute for a quit coach.
