What to do when a cigarette craving hits

The aim is not to force the craving to disappear. It is to create enough space to choose what you do next, then keep what the moment teaches you.

By Decrave Updated September 27, 2026 6-minute read

Start with the next choice, not the whole future

A cigarette craving can involve both nicotine withdrawal and cues connected to routines, emotions, places, or people. The National Cancer Institute notes that urges can come and go and that reminders associated with smoking can create a psychological pull as well as a physical one.

You do not need to solve every future craving right now. Focus on this moment: notice what is happening, choose one response that fits, and see what you learn.

The craving may still be here afterward.

That does not mean the response was pointless. You practiced having the urge without automatically following it.

Six practical responses to try

1. Name the moment

Pause long enough to ask: Where am I? What happened just before this? What am I feeling? Naming “coffee,” “stress,” “after a meal,” or “someone else is smoking” turns a vague urge into a pattern you can plan around.

2. Change one part of the situation

Move to another room, take a different break, step outside a familiar routine, or go somewhere smoking is not allowed. Smokefree recommends changing routines connected with smoking so the same cue does not always lead to the same action.

3. Slow your breathing

Try a steady inhale and a longer, comfortable exhale. Keep your attention on the physical act of breathing rather than checking whether the craving is gone. The NCI includes slow breathing among its suggestions for responding to nicotine cravings.

4. Give your hands, mouth, or attention another job

Drink water, chew sugar-free gum, take a short walk, listen to something, or begin a small task. Pick an option that is realistic in the place you are in. The point is to change direction, not to find one perfect technique.

5. Contact someone who knows what you are doing

Send a short message or make a call: “I’m having a craving and could use a distraction.” Support is more useful when the other person knows what you need, whether that is encouragement, company, or simply a different conversation.

6. Record what happened

After the moment, note the trigger, intensity, what you tried, and whether you smoked. A record can show which situations repeat and which responses you want easier to reach next time.

Match the response to the trigger

Different cravings may need different next moves:

  • Routine: change the sequence — stand somewhere else after a meal or take a different kind of break.
  • Stress or emotion: step away, slow your breathing, move your body, or contact someone supportive.
  • Social situation: prepare a simple response, ask people not to offer you cigarettes, or leave the setting for a while.
  • Nicotine withdrawal: ask a qualified professional or quitline about stop-smoking options that may be appropriate for you.

If you smoke anyway

Log the moment without erasing everything that came before it. What triggered the craving? Was your planned response available? Did the situation need more support than you had prepared?

The answer is information for the next similar moment. Your earlier progress still happened, and the next craving is another opportunity to respond differently.

When to add more support

If cravings or withdrawal are difficult to navigate, contact a quitline or qualified professional. They can discuss stop-smoking products and support options based on your circumstances. Decrave is a habit-support tool and cannot make those decisions for you.

If you are in the United States, 1-800-QUIT-NOW connects you with free quit-smoking support. Other countries offer local stop-smoking services through their public health systems.
